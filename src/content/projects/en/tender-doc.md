## Overview

This case study documents my work as a **Solution Engineer** on a 170-page enterprise tender proposal for an **Airport Operations Control Center (AOCC)** system. The source document was an English-language proposal from a large European IT vendor, and I was responsible for localizing it into Indonesian while preserving technical accuracy, terminology consistency, and the document's formal proposal structure.

Due to the confidential nature of the project, all client names, vendor names, product names, and commercially sensitive information have been removed or anonymized. What remains is a demonstration of the technical writing process itself.

## Project Context

The tender covered the implementation of an integrated airport operations platform, including:

- **Operational Database (AODB)** — flight schedule, resource planning, and real-time data management
- **Resource Management System (RMS)** — gate, stand, counter, and baggage belt allocation
- **Flight Information Display System (FIDS)**
- **Information Broker (IB)** — middleware integration layer between AODB and external systems
- **Project Management & Maintenance** — ITIL-based service delivery, SLAs, and risk management

The document contained seven major chapters spanning executive summary, technical implementation, operational procedures, facility setup, project management, and references.

## The Challenge

Translating an enterprise tender is not just a language exercise. It requires:

1. **Domain expertise** — understanding airport operations, flight data, message standards (IATA, AHM, RP), and integration architecture
2. **Terminology consistency** — using the same translated term for each technical concept across 170+ pages
3. **Structural fidelity** — preserving the hierarchical proposal format, numbered sections, and cross-references
4. **Audience adaptation** — translating for a mixed audience of technical evaluators, business stakeholders, and procurement teams
5. **Confidentiality** — handling sensitive commercial and operational information

## My Approach

### 1. Terminology Management

I built a bilingual glossary covering 150+ technical terms — from domain-specific concepts (AODB, RMS, Gantt chart, allocation rule) to standardized protocols (SMTP, LDAP, XML, XSLT). Every term was mapped to a consistent Indonesian equivalent and reused throughout the document.

For terms with no established Indonesian equivalent, I kept the English term and added a short Indonesian gloss on first mention.

### 2. Document Structure

I preserved the original numbering system (1.1, 1.2.1, 1.3.1.4.2) and mapped the document's hierarchical logic to ensure cross-references remained intact after translation.

I also added a localized **table of contents** and **figure captions** that matched the Indonesian reading flow.

### 3. Domain Research

Before translating, I studied airport operations terminology and reviewed public IATA and ICAO references to ensure accurate rendering of:

- Flight statuses (TMO, TOBT, AOBT, ATOT, etc.)
- Message types (LDM, MVT, PSM, etc.)
- Resource management concepts (stand, gate, counter, baggage belt)
- Integration architecture (adapter, broker, publish-subscribe, heartbeat)

### 4. Quality Assurance

- **Consistency checks** across chapters using a terminology list
- **Read-aloud review** to catch unnatural phrasing
- **Back-translation sampling** for critical technical sections
- **Peer review** for business-critical sections like SLAs and service descriptions

## Lessons Learned

- **Terminology consistency is non-negotiable.** A single inconsistent term can undermine credibility with technical evaluators.
- **Understand before translating.** In enterprise documentation, translating words without understanding the underlying system produces shallow output.
- **Keep the structure intact.** Proposal structure is part of the message. Don't reorganize unless asked.
- **Confidentiality is a discipline.** Sanitization must be systematic, not an afterthought.
- **Ask the subject matter expert.** A 10-minute conversation can save hours of guessing.
